How to read the chart

The chart shows the power curve: how the probability of detecting the effect varies as the sample size per group increases.

  • X axis (horizontal) — sample size per group (n). Further to the right means more users per variant.
  • Y axis (vertical) — power (1−β), between 0 and 1. The usual target is at least 0.80.
  • Each colored line represents a different effect size expressed as relative lift over p_A (+5%, +10%, +20%). Larger effects (yellow line) are detected with less sample; small effects (blue line) require many more users.
  • Vertical dashed line — appears when you calculate with a fixed n (Power and MDE modes) and marks exactly where your current sample falls on the curves.

How to use it in practice: if your n falls on the steep part of the curve, adding more users improves power significantly. If you are already in the flat zone (upper part), adding more sample barely helps. If the curves for the effect you care about are all below 0.80 at your current n, the experiment is too small to be reliable.

Key concepts before you start

These three parameters control the balance between risk, resources and sensitivity of the experiment. Understanding them is essential for interpreting any result.

Significance level α — false positive risk

This is the probability of concluding that B is better than A when in reality there is no difference. With α = 0.05, one out of every 20 experiments where no real effect exists will produce a "statistically significant" result by pure chance. Reducing α decreases false positives, but requires more sample to maintain power.

Statistical power 1−β — ability to detect a real effect

This is the probability of actually detecting the difference when it really exists. With power = 80% and a real effect equal to what you have specified, the experiment will correctly detect it 8 out of 10 times. The remaining 20% are false negatives (β): the effect exists but the test does not see it. For critical decisions it is advisable to raise power to 90% or more.

MDE — minimum detectable effect

This is the smallest absolute difference between p_A and p_B that the experiment can detect with the configured power and α. If the true effect is smaller than the MDE, the test will probably not detect it even if it exists. The MDE depends directly on n: the larger the sample, the smaller the MDE, and therefore the greater the sensitivity.

Baseline rate p_A and expected rate p_B

p_A is the current conversion rate of the control group (for example, 12% of clicks → 0.12). p_B is the rate you expect, or the minimum improvement you would want to detect in the variant. The difference \(\Delta = p_B - p_A\) is the experiment's effect.

Formulas used

For the comparison of two proportions with 1:1 allocation, the required sample size per group is:

\( n = \left\lceil \dfrac{\left(z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{2\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})} + z_\beta\sqrt{p_A(1-p_A)+p_B(1-p_B)}\right)^2}{(p_A - p_B)^2} \right\rceil \)

where \(\bar{p} = (p_A + p_B)/2\), \(z_{\alpha/2}\) is the normal quantile for the two-tailed significance level (or \(z_{\alpha}\) for one-tailed) and \(z_{1-\beta}\) is the quantile for the desired power.

Power given n is obtained with the equivalent normal approximation; in a two-tailed test both rejection tails are summed:

\( 1-\beta \approx P\!\left(\hat{p}_B-\hat{p}_A > z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{2\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})/n}\right) + P\!\left(\hat{p}_B-\hat{p}_A < -z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{2\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})/n}\right) \)

The MDE is obtained by binary search over the effect that achieves the target power with the available n.

Worked example

An e-commerce team wants to test a simplified new checkout flow (variant B) against the current flow (variant A). The current conversion rate is 12% and the team considers the change only worth implementing if it improves by at least 2 percentage points, i.e. the target rate for B is 14%. The experiment is designed with α = 0.05 two-tailed and 80% power (β = 0.20).

The auxiliary parameters are calculated. The weighted mean of both rates is \( \bar{p} = (0.12 + 0.14)/2 = 0.13 \). The required quantiles are \( z_{\alpha/2} = z_{0.025} = 1.960 \) and \( z_\beta = z_{0.20} = 0.842 \). The sample size per group formula is:

\( n = \left\lceil \dfrac{\left(z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{2\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})} + z_\beta\sqrt{p_A(1-p_A)+p_B(1-p_B)}\right)^2}{(p_A - p_B)^2} \right\rceil \)

Substituting the values:

\( n = \left\lceil \dfrac{\left(1.960\,\sqrt{2 \times 0.13 \times 0.87} + 0.842\,\sqrt{0.12 \times 0.88 + 0.14 \times 0.86}\right)^2}{(0.12 - 0.14)^2} \right\rceil \)

\( = \left\lceil \dfrac{\left(1.960 \times 0.4757 + 0.842 \times 0.4789\right)^2}{0.0004} \right\rceil = \left\lceil \dfrac{(0.9324 + 0.4032)^2}{0.0004} \right\rceil = \left\lceil \dfrac{(1.3356)^2}{0.0004} \right\rceil \approx \mathbf{4{,}461} \text{ per group} \)

The experiment requires approximately 4,461 users per group (8,922 in total). If the site receives 3,000 daily visits and 50% is allocated to the experiment, the required traffic takes about 6 days to accumulate. With a real conversion rate for B = 14%, the probability of detecting the improvement is 80%; if the real improvement is larger (e.g. 15%), the effective power will exceed 95%.

How to interpret the result

Depending on the selected mode, the calculator returns one of the three key metrics of experimental design. Each answers a different question and must be read differently.

Mode: Sample size

The calculator returns the minimum number of users per group needed for the experiment to have the desired power. For example, if you get n = 2,000, you need 2,000 users in group A and another 2,000 in group B, i.e. 4,000 in total. If you launch the experiment with fewer users, the probability of detecting the real effect falls below the threshold you have set and false negatives increase.

The result also shows the absolute effect Δ (difference in percentage points between p_A and p_B) and the relative lift (how much the rate increases in percentage terms relative to the baseline). These two numbers help judge whether the effect you are designing to detect is realistic for your business.

Mode: Power

With a fixed sample (because you already have the data or know how much traffic you will receive), power tells you what probability you have of detecting the specified difference. A power of 55%, for example, means that even if the variant is really better by the indicated magnitude, half the time the test will not detect it. If power is low, you should consider: (a) waiting longer to accumulate more sample, or (b) accepting that you will only detect larger effects.

The result also includes how many users you would need to reach 80% power, which serves as a reference for how far you still have to go.

Mode: MDE

Given the available sample, the MDE tells you what is the minimum difference you can reliably detect. If your MDE is ±3 pp and the real improvement of your variant is 1 pp, the experiment will not see it with the target power. Before launching, compare the MDE with the minimum effect that would have real business impact: if the MDE is larger, the test is not suitable for making that decision.

The result also shows the specific values of p_B that correspond to the upward and downward MDE, so you can reason in absolute conversion rate terms.

Practical recommendations

  • Power ≥ 0.80 is the recommended minimum; use 0.90 for critical decisions or when the cost of a false negative is high.
  • α = 0.05 is the standard; consider α = 0.01 if the cost of a false positive is high (launching a variant that does not actually improve).
  • Fix the sample size before launching the experiment. Stopping it before reaching n or continuing until significance is seen inflates the type I error.
  • The relative MDE is usually more relevant than the absolute: an MDE of 10% on a baseline rate of 5% equals p_B = 0.055, a difference of only 0.5 pp.
  • With limited traffic, accept detecting only larger effects (larger MDE) or extend the experiment duration rather than lowering α to compensate.

Frequently asked questions

  • Does the n calculation assume equal 1:1 allocation? Yes. The implemented formula maximizes power with balanced allocation. For unequal allocation use the general two-proportions calculator.
  • What is the difference between two-tailed and one-tailed tests? The two-tailed test detects effects in both directions (p_B greater or smaller than p_A) and is the most common. The one-tailed test only detects effects in one direction and requires less sample, but is only valid if you know in advance that the variant can only improve, never worsen.
  • Is the MDE the effect I expect or the minimum that matters? The MDE is the minimum detectable with the given sample. You should size the experiment so that its MDE is equal to or smaller than the minimum effect that would have practical relevance for your business.
  • Why does the chart show relative lifts instead of the exact effect I entered? The chart illustrates how power varies with n for three reference scenarios (+5%, +10%, +20% relative to p_A). Your specific calculation is in the results box; the chart provides visual context to understand the general sensitivity of the experiment.
