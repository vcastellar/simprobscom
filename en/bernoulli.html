Explanation

The Bernoulli distribution is the simplest discrete probability distribution, modelling a single binary trial that results in success (\(X=1\)) with probability \(p\) or failure (\(X=0\)) with probability \(1-p\). Mean \(= p\), variance \(= p(1-p)\).

It is the building block for more complex distributions: a sum of \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials is a binomial; a sequence until first success is geometric. Applications include the toss of a (possibly biased) coin, whether a single patient responds to treatment, whether a single customer converts, and the pass/fail outcome of one quality check. The Bernoulli is also the discrete uniform distribution on \(\{0,1\}\) when \(p=0.5\).

Formula

\[ P(X=k) = p^k(1-p)^{1-k}, \quad k \in \{0,\,1\} \]

So \(P(X=1)=p\) and \(P(X=0)=1-p\).

Parameters

  • p: probability of success (\(X=1\)), \(0\leq p\leq 1\).

Worked example

Situation: A spam filter classifies each incoming email independently. It correctly identifies spam with \(p=0.92\). Let \(X=1\) if the next email is correctly classified.

Question 1: What is \(P(X=1)\)?

Solution: \(P(X=1) = p = 0.92\), so there is a 92% chance of correct classification.

Question 2: What is \(P(X=0)\)?

Solution: \(P(X=0) = 1-p = 0.08\), so there is an 8% chance of misclassification. The mean is \(0.92\) (expected correct classifications per email) and the variance is \(0.92\times 0.08 = 0.0736\). If we check \(n=20\) emails, the total number of correct identifications follows \(\text{Binomial}(20,\,0.92)\).

How to interpret the result

The Bernoulli is the degenerate case with only two outcomes: \(X=0\) (failure) and \(X=1\) (success). The bar chart shows exactly two bars — height \(p\) at \(X=1\) and height \(1-p\) at \(X=0\). There are no other possible outcomes, so the PMF sums to exactly 1.

The mean \(p\) represents the long-run proportion of successes across many independent trials (law of large numbers). The variance \(p(1-p)\) is maximised at \(p=0.5\), where uncertainty is greatest, and equals 0 at \(p=0\) or \(p=1\), where the outcome is certain. For \(n\) repeated independent Bernoulli trials, use the binomial calculator instead.

Frequently asked questions

  • How is Bernoulli different from binomial? Bernoulli is a single trial (\(n=1\)); the binomial is \(n\) repeated independent Bernoulli trials.
  • What is the maximum variance? Variance \(p(1-p)\) is maximised at \(p=0.5\), giving variance \(=0.25\).
  • What does mean \(=p\) mean? Over many trials, the long-run proportion of successes converges to \(p\) (law of large numbers).
