What does a 95% confidence interval actually mean?

The correct interpretation of a 95% confidence interval is frequently misunderstood, even by researchers with statistical training. The correct statement is:

"If we repeated the experiment infinitely many times and built the confidence interval on each repetition, 95% of those intervals would contain the true parameter."

This is the statement the simulator makes visible: each row in the chart is one repetition of the experiment. The blue bars are the intervals that captured the parameter; the red ones did not. If the procedure is correct, roughly 5% of the intervals will be red.

What it does NOT mean: "there is a 95% probability that the parameter is in this particular interval." Once you've computed the interval from your data, the parameter either is inside it or it isn't. There's no probability in that classical sense: the parameter is a fixed (though unknown) value, and the computed interval is also fixed. The 95% probability refers to the construction procedure, not to the individual interval.

This distinction matters because it has practical consequences: you can't say "I'm 95% sure μ is between 47 and 53." What you can say is "the method I used to compute this interval produces intervals that cover the true μ 95% of the time across repetitions of the experiment."

The Bayesian interpretation — "the probability that the parameter lies in this range is 95%" — is valid for Bayesian credible intervals, which require specifying a prior distribution and are conceptually different.

How the simulator works, step by step

The simulator does what can never be done in practice: repeat the same study many times while knowing the true value. Each time you click Simulate:

  1. It fixes the truth. It takes the value you entered as the real one: the mean μ (and the standard deviation σ) or the proportion p.
  2. It draws a sample of size n from that population: n values from a normal N(μ, σ) in Mean mode, or n success/failure trials with probability p in Proportion mode.
  3. It builds the interval from that sample, using the chosen method, just as you would with real data: without looking at the true value.
  4. It checks whether it is right: the interval "covers" if the true value lies inside. It is drawn in blue if it covers and in red if it does not.
  5. It repeats steps 2 to 4 as many times as the number of intervals you asked for, and counts what percentage covered: that is the empirical coverage.

On the chart, each row is a different study. The central dot of each segment is that sample's estimate (its mean or proportion), and the dashed vertical line is the true value. Note that the true value does not move: what changes from sample to sample is the interval. At the 95% level, in the long run 5 out of every 100 intervals end up red, and nothing in the data of those samples warns that it was their turn.

The cards at the top summarise the simulation: the chosen nominal level, the empirical coverage, how many intervals covered and the mean width. With 100 intervals, it is normal for the empirical coverage to wobble a few points around the nominal level (91% or 98% do not indicate a problem); with more intervals, it gets closer.

Mean mode: known σ versus unknown σ

With both methods the samples are generated in the same way, from the true μ and σ you entered. The difference lies in which information each interval is allowed to use:

Known σ (z)Unknown σ (s and Student's t)
Standard deviation used by the intervalThe true σ, the same for every sampleThe standard deviation s of each sample, different every time
Critical valueNormal z (1.96 at 95%)Student's t with n − 1 degrees of freedom (2.776 with n = 5; 2.045 with n = 30)
Formula\(\bar{x} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\,\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)\(\bar{x} \pm t_{\alpha/2;\,n-1}\,\dfrac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\)
Width of the intervalsAll the sameChanges from sample to sample
Actual coverageExactly nominalExactly nominal

With known σ, the only thing that varies between samples is the centre of the interval, \(\bar{x}\): on the chart you will see segments of the same length shifted left and right. This is the textbook case, but in practice σ is almost never known.

With unknown σ, each sample also estimates the spread, sometimes underestimating it and sometimes overestimating it. That is why you will see short and long intervals mixed together. A sample that happens to be tightly clustered gives a narrow interval, which is more likely to miss. To compensate for that extra uncertainty, Student's t is used; its critical value is larger than the normal one, especially with small samples: the intervals are wider on average, and thanks to that the coverage is nominal again.

What would happen without t. If the sample standard deviation s were used with the normal critical value 1.96, a common mistake, the intervals would be too narrow: with n = 5 they would cover only 88% of the time instead of 95%; with n = 10, 92%; with n = 30, 94%. That is the whole point of Student's t.

Try it: choose n = 5 and simulate with each method. With known σ, all intervals have the same length; with unknown σ, their lengths differ a lot and the mean width is larger, but coverage still hovers around 95%. Go up to n = 100 and the two methods become almost indistinguishable.

With both methods, the simulator generates normal data. With real non-normal data, coverage is only approximate and improves as n grows, by the central limit theorem.

Proportion mode: how the Wald and Wilson intervals are built

Both intervals come from the same reasoning and only part ways at the last step. It is worth going through it slowly, because that is where the reason one works much better than the other lies.

Starting point: the sample proportion is nearly normal

If there are X successes in n independent trials and the probability of success is p, X follows a binomial distribution and the sample proportion \(\hat{p} = X/n\) has

\[ E(\hat{p}) = p, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\hat{p}) = \frac{p(1-p)}{n}. \]

By the central limit theorem, when n is large enough, the standardised proportion behaves like a standard normal:

\[ Z = \frac{\hat{p} - p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}} \;\approx\; N(0,\,1). \]

Writing \(z = z_{\alpha/2}\) for the normal critical value (1.96 at 95%), the probability that Z lies between −z and z is approximately \(1-\alpha\):

\[ P\!\left( |\hat{p} - p| \le z\,\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}} \right) \approx 1 - \alpha. \]

The confidence interval is made up of the values of p that satisfy the inequality inside. The difficulty is that p appears on both sides: in \(|\hat{p} - p|\) and inside the square root. The two methods deal with that in different ways.

Wald: replace p with p̂ inside the root

Step 1. The root is the standard deviation of \(\hat{p}\), but it depends on the unknown p. Wald replaces it with its estimate, the standard error:

\[ \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}} \;\longrightarrow\; SE = \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}}. \]

With large samples the change hardly matters, because \(\hat{p}\) gets close to p.

Step 2. The root is now a fixed number, computed from the data, and p only appears in the absolute value:

\[ |\hat{p} - p| \le z \cdot SE, \]

which is equivalent to

\[ -z \cdot SE \le \hat{p} - p \le z \cdot SE. \]

Step 3. Solving for p:

\[ \hat{p} - z \cdot SE \;\le\; p \;\le\; \hat{p} + z \cdot SE, \]

which is the Wald interval:

\[ \hat{p} \pm z\,\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}}. \]

The interval is centred on \(\hat{p}\) and symmetric. Its weak point is step 1: with small samples or p close to 0 or 1, \(\hat{p}\) is a poor estimate of the spread. The extreme case is a sample with no successes: \(\hat{p} = 0\), the standard error is 0 and the interval collapses to the single point 0, as if there were absolute certainty. And if \(\hat{p}\) is small but not zero, the lower end can come out negative and has to be truncated to 0.

Wilson: solve for p without replacing anything

Wilson keeps the unknown p inside the root and looks for all the values of p that satisfy the inequality.

Step 1. Square both sides. Both sides are positive, so the inequality is equivalent to

\[ (\hat{p} - p)^2 \le \frac{z^2}{n}\,p(1-p). \]

Step 2. Expand.

\[ \hat{p}^2 - 2\hat{p}\,p + p^2 \le \frac{z^2}{n}\,p - \frac{z^2}{n}\,p^2. \]

Step 3. Collect powers of p. Moving everything to the left gives a quadratic inequality in p:

\[ \begin{aligned} a\,p^2 - b\,p + c \le 0, \quad &a = 1 + \frac{z^2}{n}, \\ &b = 2\hat{p} + \frac{z^2}{n}, \\ &c = \hat{p}^2. \end{aligned} \]

Since a is positive, the parabola \(a p^2 - b p + c\) opens upwards and is negative exactly between its two roots. Those two roots are the ends of the interval.

Step 4. Solve the quadratic equation.

\[ p = \frac{b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}. \]

The discriminant simplifies a lot, because the \(\hat{p}^2\) terms cancel:

\[ \begin{aligned} b^2 - 4ac &= 4\hat{p}^2 + \frac{4\hat{p}z^2}{n} + \frac{z^4}{n^2} - 4\hat{p}^2 - \frac{4\hat{p}^2 z^2}{n} \\ &= \frac{4z^2\,\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n} + \frac{z^4}{n^2} \\ &= 4z^2 \left( \frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2} \right). \end{aligned} \]

Step 5. Substitute and divide by 2. This gives the Wilson interval:

\[ \frac{\hat{p} + \dfrac{z^2}{2n}}{1 + \dfrac{z^2}{n}} \;\pm\; \frac{z}{1 + \dfrac{z^2}{n}} \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}. \]

What the Wilson formula tells you

The centre is not \(\hat{p}\). Multiplying top and bottom by n, the centre can be written in two revealing ways:

\[ \frac{X + z^2/2}{n + z^2} \;=\; \frac{n}{n + z^2}\,\hat{p} \;+\; \frac{z^2}{n + z^2}\cdot\frac{1}{2}. \]

The first is equivalent to adding \(z^2/2 \approx 2\) fictitious successes and 2 fictitious failures to the sample. The second shows that the centre is a weighted average of \(\hat{p}\) and 0.5: with small samples 0.5 carries more weight, and as n grows the centre moves towards \(\hat{p}\).

The width is never zero. The \(z^2/4n^2\) term in the root does not depend on \(\hat{p}\), so even with 0 successes the interval has a sensible width.

It never leaves [0, 1], because its ends are values of p that satisfy an inequality with \(p(1-p) \ge 0\).

When n is large, it matches Wald. The terms \(z^2/n\), \(z^2/2n\) and \(z^2/4n^2\) become negligible and the formula reduces to \(\hat{p} \pm z\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}\).

It is the test turned around. The starting inequality is the non-rejection rule of the one-proportion test, which uses the standard error computed with \(p_0\). That is why the Wilson interval contains exactly the values \(p_0\) that the test would not reject with your data; it is said to "invert" the test. The Wald interval, by contrast, does not correspond exactly to that test.

A worked example

With 2 successes in 20 trials (\(\hat{p} = 0.10\)) at the 95% level:

MethodCentreInterval
Wald0.100from −0.031 to 0.231, truncated to [0, 0.231]
Wilson0.164[0.028, 0.301]

The arithmetic, step by step, with \(z = 1.96\) and \(z^2 = 3.8416\):

  • Wilson, denominator: \(1 + 3.8416/20\) \(= 1.1921\).
  • Wilson, centre: \((0.10 + 0.0960)/1.1921\) \(= 0.164\).
  • Wilson, half-width: \(1.96\sqrt{0.0045 + 0.0024}\) \(/\,1.1921\) \(= 0.137\). Interval: \(0.164 \pm 0.137\).
  • Wald: \(SE = \sqrt{0.10 \cdot 0.90/20}\) \(= 0.067\), and the interval is \(0.10 \pm 1.96 \cdot 0.067\) \(= 0.10 \pm 0.131\).

With 0 successes in 20, Wald gives the interval [0, 0], whereas Wilson gives [0, 0.161].

Neither hits 95% exactly in every case, because the number of successes can only take whole values, but Wilson always stays close. Nominal coverage vs actual coverage

The nominal level is the confidence value we specify when constructing the CI (90%, 95%, 99%). The actual (or empirical) coverage is the fraction of intervals that actually cover the true parameter across a long series of repetitions.

For the CI of the mean with a normal population, as in this simulator (both with known σ and with Student's t), the actual coverage matches the nominal level exactly for any sample size, because the sample mean is exactly normal. If the population is not normal, the match is only approximate and improves with n by the CLT.

For proportions, the situation is more complicated. The parameter p is continuous, but the binomial variable X ~ Bin(n, p) is discrete, and this discretization causes the actual coverage to fluctuate around the nominal level:

  • Wald interval: uses the normal approximation directly, \(\hat{p} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}\). It works well for large n and p away from 0 and 1, but its coverage can be noticeably below the nominal level when n is small or p is near the extremes.
  • Wilson interval: obtained by inverting Pearson's score test. It tends to have actual coverage closer to the nominal level, especially at the extremes, though it can be slightly conservative (coverage above nominal) in some cases.

You can observe this phenomenon directly with the simulator: select Proportion mode, set p = 0.05 and n = 20, and compare the empirical coverage of Wald vs Wilson.

How does sample size affect it?

The sample size n has a direct and clear effect on the width of the confidence interval. For the CI of the mean with known σ:

\[ \text{Width} = 2 \times z_{\alpha/2} \times \dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \]

The width is proportional to \(1/\sqrt{n}\): if we quadruple n, the width is cut in half. To reduce the width to a third, we need to multiply n by 9.

However, increasing n does not change the coverage: a 95% interval is still 95% regardless of whether n = 10 or n = 1000. What changes is how narrow that interval is, i.e. the precision of the estimate. Narrower intervals are more useful in practice, even though both have the same nominal coverage guarantee.

You can verify this in the simulator: keep the confidence level fixed at 95% and the number of intervals at 100, and vary n between 5 and 100. You'll observe that:

  • The fraction of blue intervals stays at roughly 95% for all values of n.
  • The intervals become progressively narrower as n increases.
  • With small n, there's more variability in the position of the CI's endpoints.

Frequently asked questions

  • If the CI doesn't contain the true value, was it calculated wrong? No. A 95% CI is designed to fail to cover the parameter roughly 5% of the time. Seeing red intervals in the simulation is exactly what's expected; it doesn't indicate any calculation error. If 95% of the intervals are blue, the procedure is working correctly.
  • Why does the Wilson method give better coverage than Wald? The Wald interval applies the normal approximation directly to \(\hat{p}\), which deteriorates when \(\hat{p}\) is close to 0 or 1 or when n is small (the distribution of \(\hat{p}\) isn't symmetric enough). The Wilson interval gets its bounds by inverting Pearson's score test, which accounts for the curvature of the binomial distribution. This produces empirical coverage much closer to the nominal level across the whole range of p.
  • Can I interpret the CI in Bayesian terms? Only if you use a Bayesian credible interval, which has a completely different construction. The frequentist CI doesn't assign probabilities to the parameter (which is treated as fixed but unknown). The Bayesian credible interval does express P(θ ∈ CI | data) = 1 − α, but it requires specifying a prior distribution over θ. Both tools are valid in their own context, but they shouldn't be confused.
  • How many intervals do I need to simulate to see the coverage converge? With 20 intervals there's a lot of variability: the empirical coverage can swing between 80% and 100% even if the procedure is correct. With 100 intervals, the standard error of the proportion is ≈ √(0.95 × 0.05 / 100) ≈ 2.2%, so the empirical coverage will typically fall between 91% and 99%. With 200 intervals, the variability drops to ≈ 1.5%.
  • Does the simulator use an exact normal distribution for the mean? Yes: in Mean mode each observation is generated from an N(μ, σ) using the Box-Muller method. The sample mean has an exactly N(μ, σ/√n) distribution for any n, so the actual coverage is exactly nominal even for small n; the empirical coverage only departs from it through simulation error.
